// ABOUT

A Master's group project built in partnership with Cornwall Council, Gallos Kernow is an educational game designed to teach local primary school children resilience when facing dangerous situations, focusing on the kinds of hazards you might find around Cornwall.

My focus was on the backend systems that held the experience together. I built a singleton GameManager to coordinate state across the project, and developed the RTS-style map alongside the UI and gameplay logic for the firefighting scene. I also implemented the score system that tracked and rewarded player progress.

Working to a real client brief gave the project a clear sense of purpose — every system had to serve the goal of teaching young players to recognise and respond to risk, not just function correctly in isolation.

// ABOUT

Galactic Awakening was my final year solo advanced game project for my Bachelor's degree, and the most ambitious thing I'd built single-handedly up to that point.

On the systems side, I developed an inventory and crafting system, ranged combat, and state-driven AI enemies. The centrepiece was a physics-based third person player controller with layered animations, splitting the upper and lower body so the character could aim and act independently of how they were moving.

I also handled the art end to end. I modelled the low poly characters — rigged through Mixamo — along with the props, vegetation, weapons and items. Owning both the code and the assets meant I could build exactly what the gameplay needed, and it gave me a real appreciation for how tightly the technical and artistic sides of a game have to fit together.

Built solo for my second year 3D level design module, this project tasked me with designing and developing a racing game across three track levels, each populated with obstacles.

I leaned on a strong toolchain to focus my effort where it mattered most. Unity's Standard Assets provided the vehicle logic and AI, Cinemachine handled the camera behaviour, and EasyRoads3D let me sculpt and paint roads directly into the terrain. For a touch of unpredictability, I dropped in an animated deer model as a free-roaming AI obstacle that turns into a ragdoll when a car ploughs into it.

I designed the UI using assets I created in Photoshop, complete with a procedurally generated speedometer. The core race logic — lap counting, racer ranking, and reset/respawn — was built on a waypoint-based system that kept everything in sync as cars moved around the track.

Roost Runner holds a special place as my first ever solo Unity project, made in the first year of my Bachelor's for a 2D level design module. The brief was open — make a 2D mobile game of our choice — so I went with an endless runner, blending a few different endless runner styles into one.

I sourced the character, enemy, tunnel, obstacle and power-up sprites from itch.io, with a handful made by myself. For enemy navigation I implemented A* pathfinding, and I developed the 2D physics-based player controller from scratch using Unity's old input system, including custom touch interaction detection for mobile.

It was my first taste of taking a game from a blank project to something playable on a phone, and it set the tone for everything I've built since.

A university game jam project from my first year, Trammered was a fast, collaborative sprint where I took on a mix of art and systems work.

I modelled the hand seen holding the beer bottle in front of the player camera, and designed and set up the menu scene — which used thrown bottles to select the menu buttons, a small interactive touch that gave the game character before play even began. I also developed the map puzzle that drove the core of the experience.

// ABOUT

Another game jam project, Dish Dasher was all about quick, satisfying arcade gameplay.

I developed the core mechanics: a player controller with simple horizontal movement driven by pointer position, and a click to swing your bat. Alongside this I built the score system, which rewarded players for stacking and smashing plates.

// ABOUT

Cyber Elite was the first game I ever developed, built for a group university project in my first year — the project that started it all.

I took on a broad slice of the work, developing the level components from the ground up: teleporters with their glitchy shader, falling platforms, level endpoints, and shock areas with their arcing shader. On top of that I built a physics-based player controller, enemy AI, a lives system, and a score system.

Being thrown straight into shaders, AI and physics on my very first project was a steep climb, but it gave me an early, hands-on grounding across the whole stack that I've drawn on ever since.
